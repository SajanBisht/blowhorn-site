"A post is won or lost in its first hour" is one of the most repeated lines in social media advice. As a rule of thumb, it is useful. As a law, it is wrong often enough to mislead you. This article covers what sits behind it, what the platforms have actually said, and what to do about it.

## How the early test works

Hypothesis: most ranked feeds work in stages. A new post goes to a small slice of your audience first. The feed watches what that slice does: replies, likes, shares, saves, time spent reading or watching, profile visits. If the response is strong, the post goes out wider; if it is flat, the feed moves on.

If that is right, early engagement carries so much weight. It is not that minute 59 is magic. It is that the first readers are the test, and a test with nobody in the room tells the feed nothing good.

## What each platform has said, and hasn't

Platforms rarely publish timing windows, so most of the numbers you will hear are observations from creators and analysts, not platform facts. The [Field Manual](/field-manual/) keeps them separate: sourced claims cite the platform, and everything else is labelled Hypothesis.

- **X.** The decisive window is commonly reported as 30 to 60 minutes, and a post that stalls early is said to rarely recover. Both are Hypothesis. What X has published is that replies count far more than likes: its 2023 ranking weights scored a predicted reply well above a predicted like, and a reply the author answers higher still. See the [X briefing](/field-manual/x/).
- **LinkedIn.** Creators talk about a "golden hour" of roughly 60 to 120 minutes, with comment velocity doing most of the work. LinkedIn hasn't published either, so both are Hypothesis. What LinkedIn has said is that it ranks on time spent reading and protects real conversation. See the [LinkedIn briefing](/field-manual/linkedin/).
- **Instagram and TikTok.** Early performance is widely reported to predict reach on both, and TikTok is described as testing videos in stages, yet posts on both can keep gaining for days. Hypothesis again. Blowhorn doesn't post to either; the [Other feeds](/field-manual/other-feeds/) page covers what they say themselves.

## What still matters after the first hour

The first hour is a strong signal, not the only one. A few things the rule of thumb leaves out:

- **Quality outlasts timing.** A post people stop on, read and reply to keeps earning, whenever it started. Dwell and replies are in the published signals; the clock is not.
- **Audiences live in time zones.** A post that starts slowly because your readers are asleep can pick up when they wake.
- **Some slow starters grow.** Shares by message and a late conversation can bring a post back. Judge a post on its full run, not its first sixty minutes.

## Give the first hour a fair test

The practical move is not to chase the first hour. It is to make sure the first hour has readers in it.

1. **Post at the audience's hour.** Schedule for when your readers are awake and scrolling, not when you finished writing. [The scheduling craft](/resources/articles/the-scheduling-craft/) covers picking those times.
2. **Be there to answer.** In X's 2023 ranking weights, replies, and especially replies the author answers, scored among the highest positive weights; X says it has adjusted them since ([X briefing](/field-manual/x/)). Schedule posts for a time you can follow up yourself, by hand.
3. **Don't stack your own posts.** Two of your posts in the same hour compete for the same first readers. Space them out; X's published ranking even discounts each post after an author's first.
4. **Never manufacture the first hour.** Pods, trades and your own accounts piling on are exactly what the platforms prohibit. The [Field Manual](/field-manual/) quotes each rule.

Blowhorn's part is the scheduling: posts go out at the time you set, at human speed, spaced the way you planned ([How Blowhorn paces itself](/docs/explanation/reliability-and-anti-bot-design/)). The engagement in the first hour has to be real, and it has to be yours.

## Read the early signal, then the whole run

When you review results, look at the first hour and the full run side by side. A post that started slow and finished strong tells you the timing was off, not the content. A post that started strong and stopped tells you the opposite. [Reading your results](/resources/articles/reading-your-results/) covers the rest of that habit.
